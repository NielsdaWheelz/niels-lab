# CANDIDATES

1. **The Ice Core** — the site is a drilled cylinder of rink ice pulled up through thirty years of resurfacings, each Zamboni pass a growth ring, with old games and old sentences trapped as bubbles you can rotate the core to find.
2. **Lightbox** — the site is a radiologist's reading room: a lit box holding films of one body, and every fracture that ever healed shows its hardware glowing gold against the grey.
3. **The Anvil Face** — the site is the struck surface of a working anvil, a topography of dings and hammer-scale build-up where each life-event is a specific dent you can press to hear what forged it.
4. **Below the Waterline** — the site is a ship's hull seen from underwater, barnacled where neglected, freshly painted in patch-shapes where repaired, riveted seams doubling as the eight lists.
5. **The Failing Scoreboard** — the site is an old arena clock whose bulbs die one at a time over real years, each dead or freshly-swapped segment a data point in a life, still keeping time correctly regardless.

# LIGHTBOX

**Epigraph:** *Nothing is so moving as an old fracture, well set.*

## THE IDEA
The site is a radiologist's lightbox holding one man's films. You land on a lit panel — a single X-ray, grey-blue, faintly humming — of a skull, or a spine, or a hand, chosen at random from a rack of eight. Down its right margin runs a typed findings column, numbered, clinical, in Quattro: this is a Shonagon list wearing a hospital's clothes. You can pull other films from the rack — click a tab labeled PELVIS, RIBS, C-SPINE, L-HAND — and each swap is a real film-changing motion, the new sheet sliding into the clips with a click. Sixty one-line entries per list, sixty numbered findings per film, eight films, one body.

## HOW THE LISTS LIVE IN IT
Each of the eight Shonagon lists becomes the numbered findings report clipped beside one film. Tap a finding line and the loupe drops onto the film at that exact anatomical point, magnifying a hairline you'd otherwise miss, and the disclosure that opens is the primary evidence — a manuscript page, a lift log, a commit. The list isn't illustrated by the body. It *is* the reading of it.

## ONE SIGNATURE MOMENT
Where an old break healed, the film shows a plate and two screws — and they are genuinely the brightest, whitest, almost-gold objects on the sheet, because titanium really is radiopaque. No filter, no glow effect: it's what an X-ray machine actually does. Kintsugi that required no invented rule, only the honest physics of the medium. That's the thing a visitor tells a friend: *his site is his own X-rays, and the repairs are the only part of him that's gold.*

## WHY ART, NOT GIMMICK
An X-ray cannot flatter. It's the one medium that refuses decoration by nature — grey, cold, correct, and it shows what a public bio never does: the cost the surfaces don't mention. That's the exact failure the current site needs corrected, solved without adding a single ornamental flourish. And film keeps accruing: new training, new pages, new commits become new incidental findings on an existing sheet, so the record is never finished, only re-read. It survives visit ten because the body underneath is still changing.

## THE FACETS
- **Hockey player** — the pelvis film, an old adductor avulsion, a hairline still faintly visible along a growth plate.
- **Strongman** — the L-spine film, Schmorl's nodes stacked like a loading log written into vertebral bone.
- **Novelist** — the skull film, whose cranial suture lines double as the site's actual section dividers — structure literally sutured.
- **Poet** — the rib film, one old fracture healed at a slight angle, so the caption reads that breath still bends around it.
- **AI researcher** — a small metallic fleck in the forearm, findings marked *etiology undetermined* — the one thing on him too new to diagnose.
- **Engineer** — the hardware itself: every plate and screw, the fixtures that hold the other five together.

## THE RISK
The failure mode is Halloween-skeleton cuteness — bones that wink, glow past what tissue does, puns that turn a body into a mascot. The rule: **nothing appears on a film that a real machine wouldn't produce.** If a hospital radiologist would call it fake, it's cut.
