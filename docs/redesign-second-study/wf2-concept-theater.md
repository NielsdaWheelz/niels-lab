1. **The Ghost Light** — the site is an empty theater at three in the morning, lit only by the single bulb stagehands leave burning for whatever is still walking the boards after everyone has gone home.
2. **The Tiring House** — the site is the backstage costume room where six roles hang on one rail, and every disclosure is the act of lifting a costume down to read the laundry tag sewn inside its collar.
3. **The Groundlings' Yard** — the site stands you in the pit among the groundlings, the stage half-blocked by a pillar, so the biography is told honestly as partial and occluded, dependent on where in the crowd you happen to be standing.

# THE GHOST LIGHT

*"Leave one lamp burning, always — the house is never as empty as it looks."*

## THE IDEA

The site is an empty theater after the last performance: house dark, curtain up on a bare stage, except one bulb on a bare stand, center — the ghost light real theaters leave burning overnight so nothing already on the boards trips in the dark. That is the whole visual grammar of the homepage: a small warm circle of light on a black field, rendered in CSS, nothing moving, no one performing. In sixty seconds a visitor reads this as an image before a site — one light, and under it, small, a name and the word *company*. Everything else — lists, log, essays, CV — sits outside the light, in the wings, reached by walking there, never by anything sliding toward you.

## HOW THE LISTS LIVE IN IT

Backstage, every theater keeps a callboard: a cork board by the stage door pinned with the night's running order, actors' calls, notices. The eight Shonagon lists become eight callboard notices — set in the existing Newsreader/Quattro pairing, index-card width, no thumbtack art, no cork texture, just the honest visual economy of a notice pinned flat. Each disclosure is a notice being unpinned and turned over, evidence stapled to its back exactly as before — byte-sacred, only re-housed.

## ONE SIGNATURE MOMENT

Past the edge of the light, in the dark lower third of the homepage, sits a sign-in sheet — the literal safety ledger a theater keeps of who is in the building. Six names, six roles. Hockey player: signed in, signed out, a year each. Strongman, novelist, poet: signed in, signed out. AI researcher and engineer: signed in — no time out. Two rows still open. That's the whole moment: a bureaucratic form, unsentimental, doing the work a highlight reel would otherwise do badly.

## WHY ART, NOT GIMMICK

A ghost light isn't decorative; it's real stagecraft superstition about the interval between shows — a theater is never truly empty, something is still walking the boards. That's a precise metaphor for a life mid-list, not concluded, and it inverts the whole genre of the personal site, which usually performs. This one refuses to perform anything — it shows what's visible when no one is on stage, the load-bearing fact under six public roles. It survives visit ten because nothing here tries to delight you twice; it's a ledger you check the way you check a callboard, changing only when the life does.

## THE FACETS

- **Hockey player** — struck. His years appear only as a callboard notice marked STRUCK, with a date: the form performs retirement rather than narrating it.
- **Strongman** — the rigging log. Theaters log every counterweight loaded onto a batten, in pounds, dated, against what it flew that season. His discipline appears as that manifest, not a photo.
- **Novelist** — the promptbook margin. Prose runs as a stage manager's annotations beside the official text: indented, italic, longer-breathed than the notices around it.
- **Poet** — the aside. Verse appears only as asides interrupting a notice mid-sentence, set off typographically, addressed to the house, undated, never gathered under a heading called Poems.
- **AI researcher** — the cue mark. Research entries are typeset as a prompter's lighting and sound cues — LX 14, SQ 9 — the annotation of the machinery, not a project list.
- **Engineer** — the medium itself. Not an entry at all: the clean semantic HTML, the cork-free restraint, the thing that keeps the show running is credited once, in the colophon, the way a Playbill credits its stage manager.

## THE RISK

The failure mode is scrapbook kitsch — twine, washi tape, a faux-handwritten font pretending at age. The rule: no stagecraft detail appears unless it names a real, specific backstage procedure with an actual name and use. If it doesn't correspond to something a real stage manager does, it doesn't render.
